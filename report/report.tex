% ============================================================
\noindent \textbf{Preliminary title:} \\
Coercion and Vulnerability: District-Level Land Use Consolidation under
Rwanda's Crop Intensification Program and Smallholder Resilience to
Agricultural and Weather Shocks.

\vspace{0.4cm}

% ============================================================
\noindent \textbf{Research question:} \\
How does district-level intensity of land use consolidation under Rwanda's
Crop Intensification Program shape the likelihood that households resort to
distress coping strategies following an agricultural or weather shock, and
does this effect vary by household wealth?

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textbf{Sub-questions:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
    \item Is district-level LUC intensity associated with a higher likelihood
          of distress coping among households that experienced an agricultural
          or weather shock?
    \item Does this effect increase as household wealth decreases?
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.4cm}

% ============================================================
\noindent \textbf{Current knowledge:} \\

Rwanda's Crop Intensification Program (CIP), launched in 2007 and ongoing,
mandates smallholder farmers to cultivate state-assigned monocrops on
consolidated plots, enforced through the \textit{imihigo} performance
contract system (Cioffo et al., 2016). The programme is widely cited as a
success, producing nationally reported yield increases---though these have
been contested on data quality grounds (Heinen, 2022). Existing research
reveals a distributional cost: Del~Prete et al.\ (2019), using EICV panel
data from 2010/11 and 2013/14, find that land consolidation participation
had ambiguous effects on dietary diversity, with no statistically significant
overall DDS effect, but significant declines in consumption of meat, fish,
and fruit. Crucially, they do not test whether these costs intensify when
households face adverse shocks. Clay and Zimmerer (2020) argue qualitatively
that the CIP eroded the crop diversity and livelihood flexibility that
previously buffered smallholders against shocks, and that this vulnerability
is unevenly distributed across wealth groups. Their claim has not been tested
quantitatively. This study addresses that gap using more recent nationally
representative data.

\vspace{0.3cm}
The core argument is that \textit{imihigo} enforcement removed the micro-level
adaptive strategies smallholders use to manage risk---intercropping, crop
diversification across moisture gradients, and flexible plot allocation---
creating structural vulnerability (Clay and Zimmerer, 2020; Matin et al.,
2018). CIP households locked into monocrops of government-selected crops,
which Clay and Zimmerer show are among the riskiest in drought and heavy
rain, have fewer options to absorb losses when shocks hit. This cost falls
hardest on the poorest households, who additionally lack savings, credit
access, and non-farm income as alternative buffers (Matin et al., 2018).

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textbf{Hypotheses:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
    \item[\textbf{H1}] Among households that experienced an agricultural or
          weather shock, those in higher-LUC districts will be more likely to
          resort to distress coping strategies than those in lower-LUC
          districts (\textit{resilience-reducing effect}).
    \item[\textbf{H2}] This effect will be larger for poorer households
          (\textit{poverty-concentration hypothesis}).
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Research design:} \\

The study matches district-level LUC intensity---constructed as a
population-weighted average share of agricultural land under consolidation
from the NISR Seasonal Agricultural Survey (SAS) 2024 across all three
seasons---to household-level data from the EICV7 (2023/24) survey. The
analysis is restricted to households that reported experiencing an
agricultural or weather shock in the past 12 months (heavy rain, floods,
landslides, drought, crop pests or disease, livestock disease, or unusually
high agricultural input prices), identified from the EICV7 household shock
module ($n \approx 3{,}000$).

\vspace{0.3cm}
The dependent variable is a binary indicator of distress coping: whether the
household's primary coping response was harmful or irreversible---reduced food
expenditure, sold land or productive assets, sold last female animals, withdrew
a child from school, or resorted to begging---as opposed to adaptive strategies
such as using savings, borrowing from a bank, or working additional hours. The
analysis uses logistic regression with LUC intensity as the main independent
variable, a LUC $\times$ wealth-quintile interaction term to test H2, and
province fixed effects and urban/rural controls throughout.
