% ============================================================
\noindent \textbf{Preliminary title:} \\
Coercion and Vulnerability: A District-Level Analysis of Rwanda's Crop
Intensification Program (CIP) and Smallholder Resilience to Shocks.

\vspace{0.5cm}

% ============================================================
\noindent \textbf{Preliminary research question:} \\
Does higher district-level land use consolidation intensity under Rwanda's
Crop Intensification Program correlate with greater household food
insecurity when households experience an adverse shock?

\vspace{0.3cm}

\noindent \textbf{Subquestions}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item Is the share of plots under land use consolidation (LUC) per
          district negatively associated with household dietary diversity
          scores among households that report experiencing an adverse shock
          in the past 12 months?
    \item Does the negative interaction between LUC intensity and shock
          exposure increase monotonically as household wealth decreases,
          indicating that the poorest households bear the greatest
          resilience cost?
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.5cm}

% ============================================================
\noindent \textbf{Preliminary knowledge:} \\

\noindent \textit{Puzzle and relevance.}
Rwanda's Crop Intensification Program (CIP), launched in 2007, is one of
the most coercively enforced agricultural transformation programmes in
sub-Saharan Africa. Under the programme's land use consolidation (LUC)
component, farmers were mandated to cultivate state-assigned monocrops on
consolidated plots, with compliance enforced through Rwanda's
\textit{imihigo} performance contract system---a cascade of accountability
running from the president through ministers and mayors down to individual
farmers (Cioffo et al., 2016). The programme produced nationally reported
yield increases for priority crops and is widely cited as a demonstration
of what strong state capacity can achieve in agricultural development
(Cantore, 2011).

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent Yet this aggregate productivity narrative conceals a distributional
question: \textit{who paid the price for these gains?} Del~Prete et al.\
(2019), using Rwanda's Integrated Household Living Conditions Survey
(EICV), find that land consolidation increased production of roots and
tubers while reducing dietary diversity---specifically decreasing
consumption of meat, fish, fruits, and micronutrient-rich foods. Clay and
Zimmerer (2020) go further, arguing through multi-season fieldwork that
the CIP reduced smallholder \textit{resilience} by stripping farmers of
the crop diversity, livelihood flexibility, and resource access that
previously buffered them against shocks. Their claim---that CIP households
are more exposed to food insecurity when adversity strikes---has not been
tested quantitatively. This study addresses that gap.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textit{Theoretical approach.}
The core argument is that Rwanda's \textit{imihigo} enforcement machinery
enabled the state to impose a macro-level productivity goal by removing
the micro-level adaptive strategies smallholders use to manage risk. The
result is a structural vulnerability: CIP households are locked into
state-assigned crops with reduced ability to diversify their diets or
adjust cultivation in response to adverse conditions---a cost that falls
hardest on those with the fewest alternative resources.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textit{Hypotheses.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item[\textbf{H1}] Households in districts with higher LUC intensity
          will show lower dietary diversity scores when they experience an
          adverse shock than households in low-intensity districts
          (\textit{resilience-reducing effect of CIP}).

    \item[\textbf{H2}] The vulnerability-amplifying effect of LUC
          intensity will increase as household wealth decreases
          (\textit{poverty-concentration hypothesis}).
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textit{Operationalization.}
The key independent variable is \textbf{district-level LUC intensity},
defined as the share of sampled agricultural plots recorded as located
within an official land consolidation site. This is constructed from
variable \texttt{s2q12} (``Is this plot located in a land consolidation
site?'') in the NISR Seasonal Agricultural Survey (SAS) 2024 plot-level
microdata, aggregated to the district level. The primary dependent
variable is \textbf{household dietary diversity score (DDS)}, constructed
from the food item consumption module of the EICV7 (2023/24) household
survey (\texttt{CS\_S8B\_Food\_Expenditure\_Consumption.dta}), by
collapsing individual food items into standard food groups and counting
the number of groups consumed. The \textbf{shock variable} is a binary
indicator derived from \texttt{s5eq1} (whether an unusual negative
situation affected the household) and \texttt{s5eq2a}--\texttt{s5eq2c}
(the nature of up to three severe shocks experienced in the past 12
months) in the EICV7 household module. The unit of analysis is the
\textbf{household}, nested within districts ($N \approx 14{,}000$
households across 30 districts).

\noindent \textbf{Methodology note:} \\
The study employs OLS regression with district fixed effects, estimated on
the EICV7 (2023/24) cross-sectional household sample matched to
district-level LUC intensity from the SAS 2024. The baseline model is:

\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{equation}
\text{DDS}_{ihd} = \beta_1\,\text{LUC}_{d}
+ \beta_2\,\text{Shock}_{ih}
+ \beta_3\bigl(\text{LUC}_{d} \times \text{Shock}_{ih}\bigr)
+ \mathbf{Z}_{ih}'\boldsymbol{\gamma}
+ \alpha_{d}
+ \varepsilon_{ihd}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\text{DDS}_{ihd}$ is the dietary diversity score for
household $h$ in district $d$; $\text{LUC}_{d}$ is the district share of
plots under land consolidation from SAS 2024; $\text{Shock}_{ih}$ is a
binary indicator equal to one if the household reported an adverse shock
in the past 12 months; $\beta_3$ is the key coefficient (a negative value
supports H1); $\mathbf{Z}_{ih}$ is a vector of household-level controls
(household size, asset index, urban/rural residence); $\alpha_{d}$ are
district fixed effects; and $\varepsilon_{ihd}$ is the error term
clustered at the district level. To test H2, the model is extended with a
triple interaction term $\text{LUC}_{d} \times \text{Shock}_{ih} \times
Q_{ih}$, where $Q_{ih}$ is the household consumption quintile.
