% ============================================================
\noindent \textbf{Preliminary title:} \\
Coercion and Vulnerability: District-Level Land Use Consolidation under
Rwanda's Crop Intensification Program and Smallholder Resilience to Shocks.

\vspace{0.4cm}

% ============================================================
\noindent \textbf{Research question:} \\
How does district-level intensity of land use consolidation under Rwanda's
Crop Intensification Program shape household dietary resilience to adverse
shocks, and does this effect vary by household wealth?

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textbf{Sub-questions:}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
    \item Is district-level LUC intensity negatively associated with household
          dietary diversity scores among households that experienced an adverse
          shock in the past 12 months?
    \item Does this vulnerability-amplifying effect increase as household
          wealth decreases, indicating that the poorest households bear the
          greatest resilience cost?
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.4cm}

% ============================================================
\noindent \textbf{Current knowledge:} \\

\noindent \textit{Puzzle and relevance.}
Rwanda's Crop Intensification Program (CIP), launched in 2007, mandated
smallholder farmers to cultivate state-assigned monocrops on consolidated
plots. Compliance was enforced through Rwanda's \textit{imihigo} performance
contract system---a cascade of accountability running from the president down
to individual farmers (Cioffo et al., 2016). The programme produced
nationally reported yield increases and is widely cited as a demonstration of
what strong state capacity can achieve in agricultural development (Cantore,
2011).

Yet this aggregate productivity narrative conceals a distributional question:
\textit{who paid the price for these gains?} Del~Prete et al.\ (2019) find
that land consolidation increased production of roots and tubers while
reducing dietary diversity. Clay and Zimmerer (2020) go further, arguing that
the CIP eroded smallholder \textit{resilience} by stripping farmers of the
crop diversity and livelihood flexibility that previously buffered them against
shocks. Their claim---that CIP households are more exposed to food insecurity
when adversity strikes---has not been tested quantitatively. This study
addresses that gap.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textit{Theoretical approach.}
Rwanda's \textit{imihigo} enforcement machinery enabled the state to impose a
macro-level productivity goal by removing the micro-level adaptive strategies
smallholders use to manage risk. The result is structural vulnerability: CIP
households are locked into state-assigned monocrops with reduced ability to
adjust cultivation or diversify diets in response to shocks---a cost that
falls hardest on those with the fewest alternative resources (savings, credit,
off-farm income).

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textit{Hypotheses.}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=2pt]
    \item[\textbf{H1}] Households in districts with higher LUC intensity will
          show lower dietary diversity scores when they experience an adverse
          shock than households in low-intensity districts
          (\textit{resilience-reducing effect of CIP}).
    \item[\textbf{H2}] The vulnerability-amplifying effect of LUC intensity
          will increase as household wealth decreases
          (\textit{poverty-concentration hypothesis}).
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3cm}
\noindent \textit{Operationalization.}
The key independent variable is \textbf{district-level LUC intensity},
constructed as the population-weighted average share of agricultural land
under official land consolidation sites across the three seasons of the NISR
Seasonal Agricultural Survey (SAS) 2024. For each season, plots are filtered
to agricultural land (\texttt{s2q7\,=\,96}), and the LUC share is estimated
as the sum of plot area $\times$ plot weight where \texttt{s2q12\,=\,1}
divided by total weighted agricultural area, then averaged across seasons.
This yields a single annual intensity measure per district. The primary
dependent variable is the \textbf{household dietary diversity score (DDS)},
constructed from the food consumption module of the EICV7 (2023/24) by collapsing food
items into standard groups and counting groups consumed. The \textbf{shock
variable} is a binary indicator from \texttt{s5eq1}--\texttt{s5eq2c} in the
EICV7 household module, equal to
one if the household reported at least one severe adverse shock in the past 12
months. Household controls (wealth quintile, household size, urban/rural
residence) are drawn from the EICV7 poverty file; savings and credit access
from \texttt{CS\_S10C\_Savings} and \texttt{CS\_S10A1\_A2\_credits} serve as
additional controls and potential mediators. The unit of analysis is the
\textbf{household}, nested within districts ($N \approx 14{,}000$ households
across 30 districts).

\noindent \textbf{Methodology note:} \\
The study employs OLS regression with district fixed effects on the EICV7
cross-sectional household sample, matched to district-level LUC intensity from
SAS 2024. The baseline model is:

\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{equation}
\text{DDS}_{ihd} = \beta_1\,\text{LUC}_{d}
+ \beta_2\,\text{Shock}_{ih}
+ \beta_3\bigl(\text{LUC}_{d} \times \text{Shock}_{ih}\bigr)
+ \mathbf{Z}_{ih}'\boldsymbol{\gamma}
+ \alpha_{d}
+ \varepsilon_{ihd}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\beta_3$ is the key coefficient (a negative value supports
H1); $\mathbf{Z}_{ih}$ includes household size, wealth quintile, urban/rural
residence, and savings/credit access; $\alpha_{d}$ are district fixed effects;
and errors are clustered at the district level. H2 is tested via a triple
interaction $\text{LUC}_{d} \times \text{Shock}_{ih} \times Q_{ih}$, where
$Q_{ih}$ is the household consumption quintile.
